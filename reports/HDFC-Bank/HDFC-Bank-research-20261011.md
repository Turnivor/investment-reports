## 0. Verdict in one paragraph

HDFC Bank is a very large, well-capitalised, low-loss bank earning an ordinary 14% return on equity, priced at about its lowest valuation in a decade (13.8x earnings, 1.8x book) because of three things at once: a deposit problem inherited from its 2023 merger that is being repaired more slowly than management promised, a governance cloud that outside reviewers and the central bank have dismissed but a US court has not yet heard, and the first outsider CEO in its history starting on 27 October. On conservative ten-year assumptions in dollars the expected return is about 8% a year, with a range of roughly 4% to 12%. That makes it **fairly priced to modestly cheap, with a free option on the franchise recovering**, not a bargain. **Conclusion: worth owning in a small, staged position; not a "back up the truck" price.** The price would become clearly attractive near ₹600 ($19 per ADR).

## 2. The business — Duan Yongping: "the right business"

**One sentence:** HDFC Bank borrows money from 100 million Indians at about 5% and lends it to households and businesses at about 9%, and loses very little of it.

**Why it was a great business.** For 25 years it had the cheapest money in Indian banking (38–48% of deposits in current and savings accounts that pay little or nothing), the lowest loan losses, and it grew 20% a year. ROE sat at 16–19% with no accidents through 2008, 2013, 2018 and Covid.

**What the merger changed.** In July 2023 it absorbed its parent, a mortgage lender with no deposits. That added a ₹6 lakh crore loan book funded by bonds and bank loans, and pushed the loan-to-deposit ratio above 100%. The plan was to replace the borrowings with deposits over three to four years.

**Where the repair stands (Q2 FY27 business update, 4 Oct 2026):**

| Measure | Pre-merger | Sep 2023 | Jun 2026 | Sep 2026 | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Borrowings (₹ lakh cr, March year-ends) | 2.57 | 7.31 (Mar 24) | 5.88 (Mar 26) | — | Improving |
| Deposit growth YoY | — | — | 14.7% | 18.8% | Strong, helped by $11.5B of foreign-currency deposits raised under an RBI swap window |
| Loan growth YoY | — | — | 15.4% | 16.3% | Accelerating |
| CASA ratio | ~44–48% | 38% | 32.3% | **31.6%** | **Still falling** |
| Loans / deposits | ~85–88% | ~107% | 96.5% | **~96.8%** | **Stalled** |
| Net interest margin (total assets) | ~4.1% | 3.4% | 3.26% | due mid-Oct | Post-merger low |

**The uncomfortable fact:** management guided to a loan-to-deposit ratio of 85–90% for FY27. Halfway through FY27 it is about 97%, because the bank chose to re-accelerate lending (16% growth) before the funding was fixed. Deposits are coming in, but as term deposits (+22.8%) at market rates, not cheap savings balances (+10.8%). The margin cannot recover until that mix turns.

**Is it still a good business?** Yes, in the sense that it earns a 1.8–1.9% return on assets with 1.17% bad loans and 17% core capital. It is no longer an exceptional one. The difference between "good" and "exceptional" is about three points of ROE and is worth roughly half the share price.

> **Duan Yongping's question:** *What is this business in one line, and has management kept to "ben fen"?*
> Cheap deposits lent carefully. The lending side is intact. The deposit side is the open question, and the 2026 allegation (below) is precisely about what the bank was willing to do to get deposits.

## 3. Moat — Buffett

| Moat | Strength | Evidence | 5-year trend |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brand / trust | ★★★★☆ | Default private bank for salaried India; one of three "too big to fail" banks designated by the RBI | Slightly down (2026 events) |
| Switching cost | ★★★★☆ | Salary accounts, largest credit-card issuer, largest merchant-acquiring network, SME current accounts | Stable |
| Network effect | ★★☆☆☆ | Merchant and payments network; modest | Stable |
| Scale | ★★★★★ | ₹32.2 lakh crore advances, ~9,000+ branches; cost-to-income ~38% | Up |
| Licence | ★★★★★ | Universal bank licences effectively closed | Stable |
| **Low-cost deposits** | ★★★☆☆ | CASA 31.6% vs ICICI ~38–40%, Kotak ~43% | **Down sharply (was ★★★★★)** |

**Net:** the moat that made HDFC Bank special, the funding-cost advantage, has narrowed from the widest in the industry to below its two best private peers. The moats it shares with every large bank (licence, scale, switching costs) are intact.

**What could rebuild it:** time (mortgage customers converted to primary-bank customers), 9,000+ branches maturing, and falling rates on term deposits. **What could prevent it:** UPI has made it effortless to keep idle balances elsewhere; every bank is fighting for the same savings accounts; and mutual-fund SIPs take money out of bank deposits every month.

> **Buffett's question:** *Will the moat be there in ten years, and what could destroy it?*
> The bank will be there and will be larger. Whether it has a funding advantage again is perhaps a coin toss. Loss of trust is the only thing that could destroy it, which is why the governance question matters more than its rupee size.

## 4. Inversion — Munger

### 4.1 How this goes wrong

| Path | Probability (judgement) | Damage | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deposit franchise never recovers; ROE stays 13–14% | 35–40% | Medium: stock is roughly fairly valued for this already | Investec already models 13–13.5% for two years |
| New CEO front-loads provisions and resets guidance | 30–40% in first 2–3 quarters | Low–medium, temporary | Common with outside CEOs; would likely be the better entry point |
| Governance allegations substantiated at senior level | 10–15% | High: regulatory restrictions, senior exits, lost trust | See 4.2 |
| Indian retail credit cycle turns | 20–25% over 3 years | Medium: this bank has the lowest losses in the system | Capital 19.6% |
| Rupee falls faster than trend | 15–20% | Direct, one-for-one on a foreign holder | Oil near $100; rupee ~₹95.5 |
| Directed lending / rate caps / deposit-rate deregulation | 5–10% | High | Not modelled in the valuation |

### 4.2 The governance question, laid out

**What is known**
- 18 Mar 2026: part-time chairman Atanu Chakraborty (a former senior finance-ministry official) resigned with immediate effect, writing that "happenings and practices" over two years were "not in congruence" with his values and ethics. He gave no specifics. The interim chairman said he had given the board no evidence.
- The RBI said the bank was well capitalised with no material concerns about governance or conduct.
- 27 May 2026: an Indian Express report alleged about ₹45 crore of payments to a Maharashtra state road corporation, booked as marketing spend, to secure its deposits.
- 26 Jun 2026: the bank disclosed that Wilson Sonsini and Wadia Ghandy, reviewing minutes and interviewing witnesses, found the ex-chairman's concerns unsubstantiated; he did not take part.
- A US securities class action names the bank and senior officers; the lead-plaintiff deadline is 13 Oct 2026. No hearing has taken place.
- 30 Jun 2026: Rajiv Kumar named part-time chairman. The CEO, Sashidhar Jagdishan, chose not to seek a third term.

**What is not known**
- What the chairman actually saw. A person of that seniority does not resign in those words lightly, and he has not withdrawn them.
- Whether the state-deposit allegation is true. Plaintiff law firms claim an internal probe held more than ten officials responsible, including the CEO. That claim appears in lawyers' advertising, not in any regulator's finding.
- Why the CEO left. The timing fits either reading.

**Two ways to weigh it.** *For the bank:* ₹45 crore is 0.06% of annual profit; the regulator that supervises it daily has stated it has no concerns; two external firms cleared it; and the board went outside the group for a CEO, which is what a board does when it wants a clean break. *Against:* a bank under pressure to gather deposits is exactly the bank that would cut this corner; a law firm hired by the board reviews what it is shown; and there was an earlier conduct lapse in the selling of Credit Suisse AT1 bonds to overseas clients (from general knowledge, not re-verified here).

This cannot be settled from outside. It can only be sized for.

### 4.3 Historical parallels

- **ICICI Bank, 2018.** CEO left amid a conflict-of-interest scandal, bad loans were high, the stock traded near 1.5x book. A new CEO rebuilt the culture; five years later it was the best bank in India and the shares had roughly tripled. *This is the bull case's template, and the new HDFC Bank CEO comes from that ICICI group.*
- **Wells Fargo, 2016.** A sales-practices scandal at America's most admired bank. The business survived easily; the regulator capped its growth for seven years and the stock went nowhere for most of them. *This is the bear case's template: not collapse, but a lost decade.*
- **Yes Bank, 2020.** Collapse. Not comparable: that was concealed bad lending and thin capital. HDFC Bank's loan book and capital are the strongest part of its story.

### 4.4 What the sellers think

Foreign institutions have cut their holding from 48.4% to 39.4% in a year, 2.43 points of it last quarter. Their case: margins at a record low and guidance on funding missed; three years of 6% EPS growth; a governance question with no end date; an untested CEO; a currency losing value; and ICICI available as a cleaner alternative. Each point is factual.

> **Munger's question:** *Where am I most likely to be wrong?*
> In assuming the old HDFC Bank is the "true" one and the last three years are an interruption. The base rate for banks that lose a funding advantage regaining it fully is not high. The valuation below therefore does not assume it.

| Mar–Oct 2026 | Chairman exit; external review; outside CEO chosen over internal deputy | Board acted, but late | B− |

**Incoming CEO: Anup Bagchi** (from 27 Oct 2026, three-year term, RBI approved 1 Oct). Three decades in the ICICI group; most recently CEO of ICICI Prudential Life, before that head of retail at ICICI Bank during its turnaround. First outsider ever to run HDFC Bank. *For:* he has lived through exactly this kind of repair, and an outsider owes nothing to past practices. *Against:* outsiders at institutions with strong cultures often lose senior people in year one (the internal candidate, the deputy MD, was passed over).

**Alignment:** no controlling shareholder; widely held; management owns little. This is a professionally run institution, so the board and the regulator are the checks. **Capital allocation:** conservative; 19.6% capital adequacy leaves room for a higher payout or for absorbing shocks.

> **Duan Yongping's question:** *If the CEO leaves, does the company keep its edge?*
> It is about to be tested directly. The lending culture has outlasted one succession already. Overall grade: **B, with the next four quarters deciding the direction.**

## 6. Civilisational trend — Li Lu

Covered at length in the industry report. In brief: India is at about $2,812 of GDP per head, bank credit is ~57% of GDP, and financial deepening follows income with great regularity. The end-state is four to six very large private banks plus the State Bank of India, and HDFC Bank is certain to be one of them on size alone. System credit should compound at 11–14% a year in rupees for a long time.

The limits: in rupees, not dollars or euros (the currency has lost roughly 3–4% a year for a decade); banking is a business where growth must be funded with capital and deposits, so a share of the growth never reaches per-share earnings; and payments, the most visible part of India's financial revolution, are a free public utility.

> **Li Lu's question:** *Looking back from 2046, is this the Standard Oil of its era or a 3Com?*
> Neither. It is closer to a JPMorgan or a Bank Central Asia: certain to exist, certain to be much larger, with the return to shareholders decided almost entirely by ROE and entry price.

Analyst targets (press reports, Aug–Oct 2026): JM Financial ₹800 · Jefferies ₹880 · Investec ₹920 (Hold) · Motilal Oswal ₹925 · Nomura ₹950 · Morgan Stanley ₹1,025 · UBS ₹1,130 · Bernstein, Macquarie ₹1,150. Every one is above the price and almost every one has been cut this year. Consensus FY28 ROE: 13–15%.

### 7.2 What the price implies

At 1.81x book with a 10% dollar cost of equity and 3% perpetual growth, the price is consistent with a sustained ROE of about 15–16% in dollar terms, or 13–14% if one accepts a lower required return. Put simply, **the market is pricing today's ROE to continue, with no recovery and no further decline.**

### 7.4 Ten-year discounted view (US dollars)

The tool's rule is that the exit multiple must come from the perpetual-growth formula, never from what peers trade at today:
Exit P/E = (1 − g / ROE) ÷ (r − g)

**Inputs and why**
- **Currency: US dollars**, because the ADR is a dollar instrument and the tool is calibrated for dollar discount rates. Rupee profit growth is converted by subtracting 3 points a year of assumed depreciation.
- **r = 10%** (dollar cost of equity, the tool's central value), with **12%** as the sensitivity.
- **Steady-state ROE:** 13% / 15% / 17%. Today 14%; pre-merger 16–19%.
- **g (after year ten):** 2% / 3% / 4%, within the dollar ceiling of 4%.
- **Profit growth to year ten (USD):** 5% / 8% / 11% (rupee 8% / 11% / 14%).
- **Starting point:** TTM consolidated profit ₹82,512 cr ≈ $8.64B; market cap $113.4B.
- **Dividend minus dilution:** 1.5% a year.

**Audit:** currency consistency ✓ · r − g between 6 and 8 points in every case ✓ · discrete risks kept out of the discount rate ✓ (tool verdict: pass, two warnings listed under limits).

| | Bear | Base | Bull |
|---|---|---|---|
| Year-10 profit (USD) | $14.07B | $18.65B | $24.53B |
| Exit P/E at r = 10% | 10.6x | 11.4x | 12.7x |
| **10-year return a year, r = 10%** | **+4.3%** | **+8.0%** | **+12.1%** |
| Exit P/E at r = 12% | 8.5x | 8.9x | 9.6x |
| 10-year return a year, r = 12% | +1.9% | +5.4% | +9.1% |

**Reading it.** On assumptions that give no credit for re-rating, the base case earns about 8% a year in dollars, below a 10% hurdle. Only the bull case clears it. The ordering of the three cases is the same at both discount rates; the absolute numbers move by 2.5–3 points, so the level depends heavily on the rate chosen. For a euro investor, add or subtract whatever the dollar does against the euro.

**Why the three-year table looks better than the ten-year one:** the three-year base case keeps today's 14x multiple; the ten-year model forces the multiple down to 11.4x as growth eventually slows. Both are legitimate. Together they say: decent, not exceptional.

## 9. Confidence, limits, and what to check next

**AI analysis confidence: high on the numbers, low on the question that matters most.** Balance sheet, margins, growth, valuation and ownership are well documented and cross-checked. Whether there was misconduct, and whether the deposit franchise recovers, are judgements no amount of public data settles. Investment certainty is therefore **medium at best**, regardless of how complete the report looks.

**Limits**
- Not modelled in the valuation (tool warnings): government-directed lending or rate caps; ADR delisting or capital controls.
- Scenario probabilities in section 4 are judgements, not statistics.
- Rupee depreciation is assumed at the ten-year trend; the pre-2016 level is from memory.
- Screener's figures are consolidated (including subsidiaries such as HDB Financial and HDFC Life); the bank-only numbers are lower (FY26 profit ₹74,670 cr against ₹79,219 cr).
- For a European holder: Indian dividend withholding tax and ADR fees reduce the 2.2% yield; check with your broker.

**Calendar**
- 13 Oct 2026: class-action lead-plaintiff deadline
- Mid-October 2026: Q2 FY27 results. Watch NIM against 3.26%, CASA, and anything said about the 85–90% loan/deposit target
- 27 Oct 2026: Anup Bagchi takes over
- January 2027: Q3 results, the first quarter under the new CEO
- April 2027: FY27 results and whatever strategy reset comes with them
